# Training level, graduation and long-term strategy

Research note for wayfinder #25: how coaches define an athlete's training level, how they detect
that the athlete has moved up, and what they change when they do. Written in the glossary's terms
(Program version, Block, Check-in, Decision, e1RM, Exposure, Warning). Claims are marked evidence
or opinion. `docs/spec/program-practice.md` covers the shape of a block and the weekly rule; this
note does not repeat it. Where the Implications below differ from `goals.md`, the spec wins
(`docs/spec/research-notes.md`).

## 1. What "level" means

Four definitions are in use. They do not agree with each other, and the coaches who use them say
so.

**By the time-scale of adaptation (Rippetoe/Baker).** A novice recovers and adapts from one
session to the next, "a process that actually takes no more than 48-72 hours"; an intermediate
needs a week; an advanced lifter a month or more. The rule they give is to "plan your training on
the previous workout if you're a Novice, the previous week's workouts if you're an Intermediate
lifter, the previous month's workouts if you're an advanced lifter" (opinion,
[SS 2018](https://startingstrength.com/article/intermediate-and-advanced-training-a-few-ideas),
[SS 2013](https://startingstrength.com/article/programming/who_wants_to_be_a_novice_you_do)). Level
is explicitly not strength: "scoring at the intermediate level in a lift doesn't change you from a
novice to an intermediate. It means you are strong for a novice"
([SS FAQ](https://startingstrength.com/get-started/faq)). Nor is it years: "anybody who has only
been training 6 months is still a beginner in terms of experience under the bar" even if no longer
a novice ([SS 2018](https://startingstrength.com/article/intermediate-and-advanced-training-a-few-ideas)).
Level is also per lift, not per athlete: "You do not need to change the programming for all lifts
– only the lift that stalls" (opinion, [Alter 2018](https://startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how)).

**By the limiting factor and its time horizon (Nuckols).** "Beginner, intermediate, and advanced
are defined in this guide by the primary factors limiting performance." Beginner "may take you
anywhere from 2-6 months"; intermediate is "generally a period of 3-8 years"; advanced starts when
"you have most of the muscle you're ever going to get" (opinion,
[SBS guide](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/complete-strength-training-guide/)). Time under the
bar is a weak proxy: in a powerlifting-meet dataset "'training age' (defined by the number of meets
completed) is barely predictive of rate of progress" (r² = 0.027), and initial strength only
matters for "the weakest ~20-30% of people" (evidence,
[SBS 2018](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=46506)).

**By years trained (the standards tables and the research literature).** ExRx: novice "up to
several months", intermediate "up to a couple years", advanced "multiple years", elite "competing
in strength sports" ([ExRx](https://exrx.net/Testing/WeightLifting/StrengthStandards)).
Strength Level: novice "at least six months", intermediate "at least two years", advanced "over
five years" ([Strength Level](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/squat/kg)). The
meta-analyses use two tiers only: trained means "weight training for at least 1 yr", untrained
"less than 1 yr of consistent training" (evidence,
[Rhea 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12618576/)); Peterson adds a third tier for
college or professional athletes ([Peterson 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16287373/)).
ACSM's position stand says novice is "no RT experience", intermediate "approximately 6 months of
consistent RT experience", advanced "years" ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)).
A proposed classification instrument scores five parameters, "current uninterrupted training
time, time of detraining, previous training experience, exercise technique, and strength level",
and its authors note "there are no objective parameters to assess training status" (evidence that
no agreed test exists, [Santos Junior 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/ssc.0000000000000627)).

**By strength relative to bodyweight.** Strength Level's levels are population percentiles of its
own users: "Beginner, Novice, Intermediate, Advanced and Elite correspond to the 5th, 20th, 50th,
80th and 95th percentiles. Your lift determines your level", and a lifter "can be Advanced at
bench press and Novice at squat" ([Strength Level FAQ](https://strengthlevel.com/faq)). Symmetric
Strength's bands are "model bands, not population percentiles", built from Wilks points, and "A
level does not measure years of training or predict when you will reach the next one"
([Symmetric Strength](https://symmetricstrength.com/learn)). ExRx's tables are "based on nearly 70
years of accumulated performance data" ([ExRx](https://exrx.net/Testing/WeightLifting/StrengthStandards)).

**Barbell Medicine rejects the framework.** "The framework is built on a biological premise that
the underlying physiology does not support." What survives is a detection window: "Training age is
the continuous variable that predicts how long an observation window has to be", and the labels
"track how often a lifter's gains are large enough to be obvious"; they tell you "nothing about the
individual's exercise prescription needs" (opinion,
[BBM 2026](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training/),
[BBM standards](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/strength-standards/)).

**Which one coaches use.** The programming coaches (Rippetoe, Nuckols, Feigenbaum, Helms) all
decide by rate of progress, and treat years and standards as background. The standards tables and
the research tiers use years because they have nothing else to measure. The two disagree on where
the boundaries fall: Nuckols's intermediate runs 3-8 years, Strength Level's starts at two years,
ExRx's ends at "a couple". No definition is validated against an outcome. The only tested claim
is Nuckols's, that training age barely predicts rate of progress, which argues against years as
the stored fact (evidence, [SBS 2018](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=46506)).

## 2. Signals that the athlete has moved up

Every source makes graduation a stall under a progression cadence, not a date or a number on a
table. Thresholds differ and are all opinion.

- **Novice to intermediate (Starting Strength).** Fail the sets and reps "for the 2nd time in a
  row", then "reset 5%" and climb back; miss "the same load a second time" after the reset and "it's
  time for a programming change" ([Alter 2018](https://startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how)).
  The FAQ allows more: "When you've done two or three de-loads and worked back up, and stall
  again, you're probably done with your linear progression for that particular exercise"
  ([SS FAQ](https://startingstrength.com/get-started/faq)). Age changes the reset: "5% if you're
  under 40 and 10% if you're over 40" ([Alter 2018](https://startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how)).
- **Novice to intermediate (Barbell Medicine).** The beginner template adds load weekly; "When
  this is no longer possible for two or more exercises, we recommend moving on"
  ([BBM forum](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/beginner-template-when-to-move-on/6357)).
  Feigenbaum resets at most once: "I would reset one time for you and then move on with
  intermediate programming after that stopped working"
  ([BBM forum 2018](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/another-linear-progression-question/1875)).
- **Beginner to intermediate (Nuckols).** "Simply stick with it until you're having a hard time
  adding weight week to week without grinding reps"
  ([SBS guide](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/complete-strength-training-guide/)).
- **Intermediate to advanced (Nuckols).** "The first time you gain less than 3-5 pounds of muscle
  ... in a year of consistent training and diet, it's time to transition to advanced training"
  ([SBS guide](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/complete-strength-training-guide/)). The
  Starting Strength marker is a rate: "athletes who are adding only 10–15 pounds to a lift over an
  8 to 10-week period"
  ([SS 2018](https://startingstrength.com/article/intermediate-and-advanced-training-a-few-ideas)).
- **Rate benchmarks for calibrating a stall.** Untrained subjects in studies gained strength at
  "2.23% per week for men"; powerlifters at "0.576 ± 1.475% ... per month", a 12.8-15.5× gap
  (evidence, [SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/predict-strength-gains/)). Meet-to-meet median
  progress fell from 0.78%/month early to 0.26%/month by the fifth meet (evidence,
  [SBS 2018](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=46506)). In a 21-week study, non-athletes gained
  20.9% in maximal force against 3.9% for strength athletes (evidence,
  [Ahtiainen 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12734759/)). MacroFactor's rule of thumb:
  beginners "add weight to the bar every workout", intermediates "most workouts", experienced
  lifters have "slowed down to well below 1% per week" (opinion,
  [MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/)).
- **A flat e1RM over a block is normal for a trained lifter.** Baraki, to a lifter whose e1RMs
  flattened after about six weeks on each program: "is your expectation that e1RMs should trend
  upwards continuously, indefinitely? This seems to be completely normal", and six weeks "is
  likely good timing for you to do a low stress week and change the programming stimulus"
  (opinion, [BBM forum 2020](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/often-stop-responding-to-programs-and-regress-after-about-6-weeks/8091)).
- **Recovery not keeping up** is in the definition (novice recovers in 48-72 h) but no source gives
  a measurable recovery threshold for graduation. BBM's plateau definition is the general one:
  "a period where measurable progress in strength no longer occurs despite continued training"
  ([BBM](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/strength-training-plateau/)).

The concrete rules reduce to one shape: N misses at a load, R resets allowed, then the cadence
changes for that lift. Starting Strength gives N = 2, R = 1 (Alter) or 2-3 (FAQ); BBM gives R ≤ 1
and "two or more exercises" stalled at once. Nothing is validated.

## 3. What changes at each graduation

**Progression cadence.** Session → week → month, by definition (section 1). At the handover
"progression moves from 48 hours to weekly"
([SS 2018](https://startingstrength.com/article/intermediate-and-advanced-training-a-few-ideas)).
Helms's published programs match: a novice program adds "weight in steady increments each time",
an intermediate one wave-loads across the week (opinion, Helms Boostcamp program pages, via the
source agent; the Muscle and Strength Pyramid text itself was not obtained).

**Periodisation.** Periodised beats non-periodised for strength by a small margin: ES 0.43
[0.27, 0.58] over 18 studies, with "periodization model", "training status" and frequency as
moderators (evidence, [Williams 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/)); an independent
analysis gives ES 0.31 [0.04, 0.57] for 1RM and no hypertrophy effect (evidence,
[Moesgaard 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)). Whether the
margin is bigger in trained lifters is contested. Nuckols's pooling: periodised vs not is d 0.23-0.30
overall, 0.31-0.47 in trained; undulating vs linear is trivial in untrained (d < 0.12) and medium
in trained (d 0.56-0.76, about 28% faster) ([SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/periodization/)).
Moesgaard agrees on undulating in trained lifters (ES 0.61 [0.00, 1.22]). But Williams's own
moderator runs the other way: "Improvements in 1RM were greater among untrained participants"
([Williams 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/)). The two linear-vs-undulating
meta-analyses found no overall difference for strength ([Harries 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268290/))
or hypertrophy (d −0.02, [Grgic 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28848690/)). Safe reading:
periodise everyone; add undulation once the athlete is trained; do not expect a large effect.

**Dose.** The strongest training-status evidence is the dose-response work: "training status
influences the requisite doses as well as the potential magnitude of response" and "an increase in
resistance-training dosage must accompany increases in training experience". Untrained: 60% 1RM,
3 d/wk, 4 sets per muscle; recreationally trained: 80%, 2 d/wk, 4 sets; athletes: 85%, 2 d/wk,
8 sets (evidence, [Peterson 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16287373/),
[Rhea 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12618576/)). Intensity rises with status; set count
rises only at the athlete tier. ACSM says the same in prescription form: novices at 8-12 RM,
intermediate and advanced "1 to 12 RM in a periodized fashion with eventual emphasis on heavy
loading (1-6 RM)", frequency 2-3, 3-4 and 4-5 d/wk by level ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)).

**Volume.** More weekly sets give more hypertrophy, with diminishing returns: +0.37% per set
(evidence, [Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/)); +0.24% per fractional
set for hypertrophy and +0.21% for strength with "strong diminishing returns and a functional
plateau" for strength, from 67 studies (evidence, [Pelland 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)).
Pelland adjusted for training status and did not find that trained lifters need more sets; its
efficiency tiers (about 6 extra weekly sets for a detectable gain at 5-10 sets, 8.5 at 11-18,
10.75 at 19-29) are the same for everyone. Nuckols's view that optimal volume is "low for totally
untrained lifters, considerably higher for moderately trained lifters" is his hypothesis, and he
names the confound: high-volume lifters "generally have more prior training experience"
(opinion, [SBS volume](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/volume/)). Ralston found low weekly sets
no worse for strength in novices but medium or high appropriate for "well trained individuals"
(evidence, [Ralston 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28755103/)).

**Deloads.** The Delphi panel reached 100% on "volume and intensity may change depending on
experience, age, and level of performance" but no statement that novices need none
([Bell 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)). The one RCT in trained lifters
found a planned mid-block deload gave no hypertrophy difference and less strength than training
through (evidence, [Coleman 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)). Deloads are a
fatigue tool, not a level marker.

**Variation.** "Some degree of systematic variation seems to enhance regional hypertrophic
adaptations and maximize dynamic strength, whereas excessive, random variation may compromise
muscular gains" (evidence, [Kassiano 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)). No source
ties the amount of variation to level; "novices need less variation" is unsupported.

## 4. Long-term strategy for strong and jacked

**Sequence or concurrent.** Israetel's powerlifting plan is sequential: hypertrophy (5-10 reps)
→ strength (3-6) → peaking (1-3), on the claim that "Training in a certain style one month can
enhance the gains made with another style next month"; intermediates "may better spend their time
equally between the hypertrophy and strength phases", about 1:3:1 for an advanced lifter
(opinion, [JTS](https://www.jtsstrength.com/nonsense-periodization-powerlifting/)). JTS ranks
this "Phase Potentiation" its sixth principle of seven (opinion,
[JTS](https://www.jtsstrength.com/principle-of-phase-potentiation/)). Feigenbaum: "there is a time
and place for both of them, but I also don't think they're necessary for everyone"; BBM's
powerbuilding template runs "two 5-week training blocks and a final testing week" with both
qualities in every block (opinion, [BBM forum](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/question-about-training-template-progression/11377)).

**Evidence for sequencing.** Thin. Block beat undulating in track athletes on efficiency with
"no statistically significant differences" in outcome ([Painter 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22173008/));
block vs traditional in trained men changed the force-power curve with "No significant changes
... in lower-body strength" ([Bartolomei 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24476775/)). The
"resensitisation" idea, a month "at or near MV" to lower "adaptive resistance"
([Israetel](https://drmikeisraetel.com/e-book-scientific-principles-of-hypertrophy-training/)),
rests on detraining studies: 3-week off, 6-week on cycles matched continuous training at 24
weeks (evidence, [Ogasawara 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/)). What the loading
evidence does support is that both qualities can be trained in one block: "maximal strength
benefits are obtained from the use of heavy loads while muscle hypertrophy can be equally
achieved across a spectrum of loading ranges" ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/));
heavy beats light for strength (SMD 0.60-0.63) and no load wins for hypertrophy
([Lopez 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/)). So "strong and jacked" is heavy work
on the chosen lifts plus enough sets elsewhere, in every block, which is what ADR 0009 already
says powerbuilding is.

**When the next phase starts.** Three triggers appear: a planned block count (JTS: the meet
date fixes the whole cycle; BBM: 5-week blocks), a stall (Baraki: six weeks flat, then a low-stress
week and a new stimulus), and a body-composition phase change. No source makes level the
trigger.

**Body-composition phase sets the emphasis.** Israetel: "Mass phases are characterized by high
volume training and hypercaloric dieting", "Cut phases are characterized by high volume training,
hypocaloric diets", maintenance "is mostly characterized by basic strength and peaking training";
mass and cut phases "generally last between one and three months" (opinion,
[JTS](https://www.jtsstrength.com/the-terms-of-the-deal/)). Helms's contest-prep review agrees
on keeping volume in a deficit: "most repetitions should occur in the 6-12 range using 70-80% of
1 repetition maximum", "Roughly 40-70 reps per muscle group per session", periodised, with
weight loss "approximately 0.5 to 1%/wk to maximize muscle retention" (evidence as review,
[Helms 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24998610/), [Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)).
Feigenbaum differs in emphasis, not substance: "good strength-based programming that is periodized
over time should be considered independent of nutrition most of the time", with strength gains on
a cut "slower than ... during weight gain for most folks" (opinion,
[BBM forum](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/training-during-caloric-deficit/2917)). Gain
rates: 0.5-1.5% of bodyweight per month (Helms), 0.15-0.8% per week for an intermediate
(MacroFactor), "0.5 to 2 lbs of lean mass per month at most" (RP) (all opinion,
[Helms](https://legionathletics.com/eric-helms-interview-lean-gains/),
[MacroFactor](https://macrofactor.com/bulking-calculator/),
[RP](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/videos/are-you-actually-gaining-muscle-here-s-how-to-know)).

## 5. Reading a milestone as a level

The milestones in the ticket (deadlift 2×BW, squat 1.5×BW, bench 1.25×BW, press 0.75×BW) land
on "intermediate" on every table, for a man of about 80-90 kg. Figures are 1RM, bodyweight
multiples computed here.

| Lift | Milestone | Strength Level (male) | ExRx 82 kg, int / adv | Symmetric Strength 80 kg, int / prof / adv |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Squat | 1.5× | between novice 1.25 and intermediate 1.75 | 1.49 / 2.04 | 1.51 / 1.90 / 2.21 |
| Bench | 1.25× | intermediate 1.25 | 1.10 / 1.52 | 1.14 / 1.41 / 1.65 |
| Deadlift | 2.0× | intermediate 2.0 | 1.74 / 2.44 | 1.75 / 2.18 / 2.54 |
| Press | 0.75× | just under intermediate 0.80 | 0.76 / 0.91 | 0.74 / 0.93 / 1.08 |

Sources: [Strength Level](https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/squat/kg) (percentiles
of its users, male 1.25/1.75/2.25 squat; 1.00/1.25/1.50 bench; 1.50/2.00/2.50 deadlift;
0.55/0.80/1.05 press for novice/intermediate/advanced); [ExRx](https://exrx.net/Testing/WeightLifting/StrengthStandards)
(82 kg men 18-39: squat 122.5/167.5, bench 90/125, deadlift 142.5/200, press 62.5/75);
[Symmetric Strength](https://symmetricstrength.com/standards) (80 kg: squat 121/152/177, bench
91/113/132, deadlift 140/174/203, press 59/74/86). Strength Level sits highest because its
population is self-selected loggers, which it admits ([FAQ](https://strengthlevel.com/faq)).
Symmetric Strength applies "an age adjustment when the lifter is under 23 or over 40"
([about](https://symmetricstrength.com/about)); ExRx does not.

Reading: the four milestones together describe an ExRx or Symmetric Strength intermediate, the
bench slightly ahead and the squat slightly behind the deadlift. "Advanced" on those tables is
about 2×BW squat, 1.5×BW bench, 2.5×BW deadlift, 0.9-1.1×BW press. All three tables say level is
per lift. None claims the label predicts how the athlete should train (section 1).

## 6. Reviewing progress against a goal

**What is measured.** Lifts: e1RM trend and reps at a load; RP's first test for muscle gain is
"if your performance in the gym is improving" ([RP](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/videos/are-you-actually-gaining-muscle-here-s-how-to-know)).
Body: daily weigh-ins reduced to a trend; Helms compares "two seven day averages compared to your
last two seven day averages" when cutting and a 14-day average against the next 14 when gaining
(opinion, [Helms](https://legionathletics.com/eric-helms-podcast/),
[Helms](https://legionathletics.com/eric-helms-interview-lean-gains/)); MacroFactor's trend
weight is "a moving average ... that places greater emphasis on more recent weigh-ins"
([MacroFactor](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/dashboard/weight_trend/)). Photos "6-8 weeks
apart" (RP). Adherence, sleep, hunger and stress are the check-in questions.

**Cadence.** Weekly for the numbers, two weeks before a diet change ("probably two weeks before
making changes", a "100 to 200 calorie cut", Helms), a block for the program. The ladder when a
block stalls, from Israetel: "my first recommendation is deload", then "do more" volume, then more
frequency, then "try the other ranges" (opinion, [Israetel](https://legionathletics.com/mike-israetel-interview/)).
BBM's order is the same as `program-practice.md` records: volume before a new exercise; and a flat
six weeks is a block boundary, not a crisis (section 2). Helms's order for a stalled cut is
adherence and bodyweight trend first, then performance ("am I maintaining performance?").

**What nobody does.** No source re-labels the athlete's level at a review, or consults a standards
table to decide the next block.

## Implications for the domain model

**Training level is a derived signal per lift, not a stored fact and not an athlete attribute.**
Every programming coach defines it by rate of progress on a lift, the one tested claim says years
barely predict that rate (evidence, section 1), and Starting Strength, BBM and all three standards
tables make it per lift. `coach.toml` `[athlete].experience` (ADR 0009, `instance.md`) is a
years-or-label field a rule reads. Either drop it or keep it as prose context in the Profile.
Nothing in this note supports a formula that reads it (opinion).

**The code rule is a cadence stall, which the registry already has.** The graduation signal
coaches use is "N misses at a load after R resets on this lift", which is the `stall_streak`
warning in `validation.md` with two parameters added: the resets taken under the current
progression scheme and the rate of e1RM change over the block (evidence that the shape is
shared: section 2; the thresholds N = 2, R = 1-3, "two or more lifts" are opinion). A second,
block-scoped signal is the one Baraki names: e1RM flat across the whole block on a lift. Both are
warnings, never violations, because the sources say a flat block is normal for a trained lifter.
Neither should carry the word "level".

**Graduation is a Decision with a reason.** What changes at graduation is the progression
scheme on that line (session → week → block cadence; linear → undulating) and the dose, which
are Program version fields. So "the athlete has moved up on the squat" is recorded as a new
Program version cut at a block boundary, with the Decision's reason citing the stall warning.
That is the same promotion path `program-practice.md` gives a persisting exercise swap. No
separate level record is needed (opinion, consistent with ADR 0009's rule that a judgement with
a reason is a Decision).

**The milestone is a Follow-up, read against a table once.** ADR 0009 already makes a dated
milestone a Follow-up. Section 5 shows a milestone maps to a level only loosely and only per
lift; the engine can render "deadlift 2×BW: 0.3× to go" from weigh-ins and e1RM, but the
"this is intermediate" reading is prose the coach writes, since the tables disagree by 0.25×BW
and none predicts programming (opinion).

**A typed long-term plan is not what coaches use.** The sources hold three kinds of long-range
structure: a block sequence fixed by a competition date (JTS, strength sport only), a
body-composition phase with a start, an intended rate and an emphasis (Israetel, Helms), and
"next block changes the stimulus" (BBM). Only the body-composition phase is a thing that outlives
a block and changes what the week is checked against: it sets the expected bodyweight trend, the
dose floor in a deficit, and the gain-rate band. The evidence that sequencing blocks by quality
helps is weak (section 4), so a typed "strategy" of ordered future blocks would encode a
convention the research does not back and the athlete's own coaches (BBM) do not require. If
anything is typed it is **Phase**: kind (deficit, maintenance, surplus), start, target rate as
%BW/week, until date or until condition, and the Emphasis it implies; a Decision
opens and closes one. The Program version keeps the emphasis (ADR 0009, ADR 0014). The sequence of
future blocks stays in the Profile prose and in Follow-ups (opinion).

**What stays the coach's judgement.** Whether a stall is a graduation, a fatigue problem, or a
diet problem (Israetel's ladder and Helms's adherence-first order are both judgement calls with
no thresholds); whether to change cadence on one lift or the whole program; when a
body-composition phase ends. The engine states the facts (stall streak with resets, block e1RM
slope, bodyweight trend against the phase's target rate, adherence) in the check-in's compact
summary, and the coach's read says what they mean.

**Open.** The Muscle and Strength Pyramid level guidelines, Practical Programming's own text and
Santos Junior's scoring cut-offs were not obtained; the sites block fetches or are paywalled. None
would change the conclusions above, but the N and R thresholds would gain one more source.
